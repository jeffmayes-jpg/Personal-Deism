All Sentient Creatures, Even If Not A Person, Have Five Freedoms

Freedom from hunger and thirst

Access to fresh water and a proper diet.

Freedom from discomfort

Appropriate shelter and resting areas.

Freedom from pain, injury, or disease

Prevention or rapid treatment.

Freedom to express species-appropriate behavior and natural capacities

Adequate space and companionship.

Freedom from fear and distress

Conditions that prevent mental suffering.


The Five Freedoms describe morally significant conditions of well-being rather than identical forms of autonomy.


"Freedom" in this context does not require that every creature possess human-like agency, rationality, or political liberty.


The freedoms establish protections against unnecessary deprivation, suffering, fear, and interference with species-appropriate functioning.


The particular application of each freedom depends upon the nature, needs, capacities, and circumstances of the creature concerned.


In Personal Deism, animalhood is a biological classification, sentience establishes morally significant interests and five freedoms, and personhood is established by the relevant capacities for consciousness, self-awareness, rationality, and morally significant relationship—not by species membership alone.


Legal ownership establishes responsibilities and lawful authority over care; it does not erase the creature's morally significant interests.


Sentience creates the capacity for subjective welfare and suffering. Because unnecessary deprivation, suffering, fear, and frustration of species-appropriate capacities can directly affect the welfare of a sentient creature, these conditions constitute morally significant interests. The Five Freedoms identify basic categories of protection corresponding to those interests.


Question

How is sentience established?


Response

Sentience is established through observation of Behavioral Responses, Physiological Structures, Evolutionary Context, and Valence and Subjectivity.


Question

Why precisely these five?


Response

The Five Freedoms identify fundamental conditions necessary for the welfare of sentient creatures: adequate nourishment and hydration, freedom from environmental discomfort, protection from pain, injury, and disease, opportunity to express species-appropriate capacities, and freedom from fear and distress.

These categories correspond to the principal forms of deprivation or suffering that can directly compromise the welfare of a sentient creature. They are intended as fundamental categories of protection rather than as an exhaustive list of every morally significant interest a creature may possess.

The Five Freedoms are therefore not arbitrary. They arise from the morally significant interests created by sentience and from the conditions necessary for a sentient creature to experience a minimally adequate state of well-being. Their particular application depends upon the nature, capacities, needs, and circumstances of the creature concerned.

The Five Freedoms also do not establish identical requirements for every creature. A freedom is applied according to the creature's nature. What constitutes appropriate nourishment, shelter, natural behavior, companionship, or protection from distress will differ among species and among individual circumstances.

The five freedoms are therefore best understood as fundamental categories of protection, not as five rigid rules or an exhaustive enumeration of all possible moral interests.


Question

Why does Personal Deism use the word "freedom" for the five freedoms?


Response

The five freedoms use "freedom" in a broader sense than political liberty.


They describe conditions in which a creature is able to live without unnecessary deprivation, suffering, fear, or restriction of its normal behavior.


They therefore include both negative and positive dimensions of freedom.

Negative freedom concerns protection from unnecessary interference, injury, coercion, deprivation, or suffering.

Positive freedom concerns access to the basic conditions necessary for the expression of normal capacities.


The five freedoms are therefore:

1. Freedom from hunger and thirst.

2. Freedom from discomfort.

3. Freedom from pain, injury, or disease.

4. Freedom to express species-appropriate behavior and natural capacities.

5. Freedom from fear and distress.


These freedoms are not unlimited licenses of action.


They establish presumptive protections against unnecessary deprivation and suffering.


Where legitimate interests conflict, the freedoms must be reconciled according to necessity, proportionality, justice, responsibility, and prevention of serious harm.


Question

If all sentient creatures possess five freedoms, do all sentient creatures possess rights?


Response

Not necessarily in the same sense.


Personal Deism distinguishes between morally significant interests, protected freedoms, rights, and legal rights.


All sentient creatures possess morally significant interests that give humans and other morally capable agents reasons to avoid unnecessary suffering and deprivation.


The five freedoms therefore establish fundamental protections for creatures even when those creatures are not persons.


Personhood establishes a distinct category of moral standing. Persons possess intrinsic dignity and therefore possess equal basic rights appropriate to persons.


The distinction is:

Creature → morally significant interests and five freedoms.

Person → intrinsic dignity, moral standing, and basic rights.

Citizen → legal rights recognized and protected by legitimate government.


Equal dignity applies equally to all persons. The five freedoms do not imply that every creature possesses identical rights, political status, or authority over its own circumstances.


The absence of personhood does not make a creature morally irrelevant.


The possession of personhood does not make all interests unlimited.


Question

Can a creature's five freedoms be restricted?


Response

Yes, when necessary and proportionate.


A restriction may be justified when necessary to prevent serious harm, protect another creature or person, provide necessary medical care, prevent dangerous behavior, or address unavoidable conflicts between legitimate interests.


A sufficiently serious public interest is one whose protection is necessary to protect fundamental rights, public safety, or the legitimate conditions of peaceful coexistence and which can be justified by publicly examinable reasons.


The justification must remain proportionate to the interest being protected.


The fact that a creature is not a person does not provide unlimited permission to cause suffering.

The five freedoms therefore establish presumptive protections rather than absolute permissions to act without regard to circumstances. Their application must remain sensitive to the creature's actual capacities and welfare rather than imposing a human model of autonomy.


Question

Do you have legal ownership of an animal?


Response

No. You may bear legal responsibility for it, if it is under your care, but that in no way means that an abridgment of its freedoms may be imposed. Legal ownership establishes responsibilities and lawful authority over care; it does not erase the creature's morally significant interests. That said, some of the above freedoms may be reasonably curtailed to prevent abuse or harm to others, if you are legally responsible for that animal.
