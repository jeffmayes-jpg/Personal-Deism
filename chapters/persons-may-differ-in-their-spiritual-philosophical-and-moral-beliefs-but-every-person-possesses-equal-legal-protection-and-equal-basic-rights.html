Persons may differ in their spiritual, philosophical, and moral beliefs, but every person possesses equal legal protection and equal basic rights.


Society flourishes when individuals act with empathy, justice, mercy, and respect for others.


Persistent, serious violations of others' freedoms after due process may ultimately justify removal from society.


Any restriction of liberty must remain subject to:

due process
necessity
proportionality
least-restrictive means
periodic review
equal dignity
rehabilitation/reintegration where reasonably possible.


Equal basic rights include:

  1. Equal legal protection

Every person is entitled to equal protection under the law, regardless of their beliefs or other distinctions.

  1. Equal basic freedom / liberty
    Every person possesses equal basic freedom and that one person's freedom cannot include unlimited authority to deprive another person of equivalent freedom.

  2. Freedom of belief
    People may differ in their spiritual, philosophical, and moral beliefs without losing their equal legal protection or basic rights.

  3. Protection of intrinsic dignity
    Because dignity is intrinsic and equal, legitimate authority cannot treat one person's dignity as inherently less valuable than another's.

  4. Protection against unjust coercion
    Government does not have authority simply to compel people to become virtuous. Coercion requires a legitimate justification grounded in rights, justice, or sufficiently serious threats.

  5. Bodily/physical safety
    Physical safety is a morally significant interest and permits protective force only when necessary and when no less-restrictive means is reasonably available.

  6. Freedom of agency / personal choice

Freedom establishes personal agency. A person's legitimate choices therefore receive protection unless exercising that freedom violates the legitimate rights or freedom of another.

  1. Protection from arbitrary discrimination in intrinsic worth
    Because dignity is explicitly equal regardless of ability, status, wealth, intelligence, health, circumstance, belief, achievement, or social contribution, none of those distinctions can legitimately establish differences in fundamental personal worth.


These rights are not necessarily an exhaustive enumeration. Additional basic rights may be recognized when they are necessary to protect equal dignity, fundamental agency, bodily integrity, legitimate freedom, or the conditions required for persons to exercise their existing rights. Any proposed right must be justified by the same principles of equal dignity, universalizability, necessity, and protection against unjust coercion.


Principle

Legitimate coercion must be proportionate to the legitimate interest being protected.


Government may restrict a person's freedom only when:

1. A legitimate right or sufficiently serious public interest is at stake.

2. The threatened harm is sufficiently serious to justify coercion.

3. The restriction is reasonably related to preventing or addressing that harm.

4. No substantially less restrictive alternative is reasonably available.

5. The restriction is proportionate to the harm being prevented.

6. The restriction does not arbitrarily deny the equal dignity of the person being restricted.


Principle

Protective Force Is a Last Resort


Protective force is justified only when:

1. A person faces a sufficiently serious threat to bodily safety, fundamental rights, or equivalent legitimate interests.

2. The threat is reasonably foreseeable or presently occurring.

3. The person using force has a legitimate protective interest.

4. The force is reasonably necessary to prevent or stop the threat.

5. No substantially less restrictive alternative is reasonably available.

6. The force used is proportionate to the threat.

7. The purpose of the force is protection rather than punishment, domination, revenge, or personal gain.


Mere disagreement, offense, criticism, insult, or rejection of another person's beliefs does not by itself justify protective force.


Force may be used to protect a person from serious rights violations, but it may not be used simply to compel conformity, virtue, belief, or ideological agreement.


Question

When may force be used?


Response

Force may be used as a last resort when necessary to protect a person from sufficiently serious harm or violation of fundamental rights and when substantially less restrictive means are not reasonably available.


The justification for force derives from the protection of legitimate rights, not from the superiority of the person exercising force.


Government therefore possesses protective authority, not unlimited moral authority.


The existence of a moral obligation does not by itself establish a governmental power to enforce it. Governmental coercion additionally requires legitimate jurisdiction, due process, public justification, necessity, and proportionality.


Objection

Justice is more important than compassion.


Response

Compassion does not replace justice; it informs how justice is exercised. Justice protects flourishing, while compassion guides its application. Justice governs rights; compassion governs their humane application. Justice without compassion is mechanical.
