The Divine Exists, Is Intentional And Good, And Persons Possess A Genuine Ontological Relationship To The Divine


Reality includes a transcendent or deific aspect that is neither reducible to matter nor merely a personal invention.


Within Personal Deism, recognition is understood to aid moral growth. Recognition of the deific aspect is not necessary in order for the individual to thrive, but greatly aids in that person’s growth. Recognition is not merely mentally knowing, but intentionally acting in such a manner that the action points to that aspect.


Attribution to the divine is explicit action or speech that points to the divine as the source, and is definitional within Personal Deism.


Recognition of the divine does not automatically confer attribution to the divine. While the recognition is laudable, attribution to the divine is preferable.


It is entirely possible to recognize the divine and live a virtuous existence, but the fullest realization of one's potential is aided by attributing the divine as the source. The legal order protects the conduct and dignity of citizens, and does not rank their metaphysical interpretations.


Personal Deism does not claim that the divine is fully comprehensible. Human concepts and language can refer to and disclose aspects of the divine without exhausting the divine.


The distinction between incompleteness and unknowability is important: the inability to comprehend the divine exhaustively does not imply that nothing about the divine can be known.


Question

Is the Divine a Person?


Response

Yes. Personal Deism applies its established criteria for personhood to the Divine. The Divine is understood to possess consciousness, self-awareness, rational capacity, and the capacity to participate in morally significant relationships. The Divine therefore qualifies as a Person. The Divine is not exempt from the test merely because it is Divine.


Objection

If the goodness of the divine is simply declared to be a foundational commitment, is morality arbitrary?


Response

No.


A foundational commitment is not necessarily an arbitrary preference. It is a proposition accepted as a starting point for the system rather than derived from propositions that precede it within the system.

Personal Deism does not claim that the divine is good merely because the philosophy says so. It claims that goodness belongs necessarily to the nature of the divine.


The proposition is therefore metaphysical rather than conventional.


The distinction is:

Arbitrary commitment:

"The divine is good because we choose to call the divine good."

Foundational metaphysical commitment:

"The divine is good because goodness belongs necessarily to the nature of the divine."


The second claim remains open to philosophical criticism, but it is not equivalent to the first.


Personal Deism therefore does not regard morality as arbitrary divine command. Moral goodness is grounded in the nature of the divine rather than created by an act of divine preference.


Objection

There is no evidence that the divine exists.


Response

Personal Deism accepts empirical inquiry but does not limit reality to what empirical methods alone can detect. Rational inquiry and spiritual experience are both legitimate avenues toward truth.


The existence of a naturalistic description of the mechanisms accompanying an experience does not by itself establish that the experience has no transcendent significance. The epistemic question is whether the proposed explanation adequately accounts for the experience and what, if anything, follows from it.


Objection

Spiritual experience is subjective.


Response

Subjectivity alone does not invalidate an experience. Spiritual insight complements, rather than replaces, rational investigation. Spiritual experience can provide prima facie justification for the person who experiences it, but it remains open to evaluation, interpretation, and possible defeat by stronger evidence.


A spiritual experience may rationally contribute to personal belief without automatically creating a public obligation for others to accept the same interpretation.


Private spiritual insight cannot override another person's equal rights.


A spiritual interpretation that requires cruelty, domination, denial of equal dignity, or rejection of well-established evidence is presumptively defective within Personal Deism.


Experience provides evidence, but evidence is not infallible.


Human language and concepts can point toward ultimate reality but cannot exhaustively describe it. The answers given point to, but do not encompass, the divine.

Question

Is the universe itself divine?


Response

The universe participates in and expresses the divine, but the universe is not identical with the divine. In this sense, the universe possesses a genuinely divine character because it exists within the divine.


Personal Deism is therefore panentheistic rather than pantheistic.


Pantheism identifies God and the universe.


Panentheism holds that the universe exists within the divine while the divine is greater than and not exhausted by the universe.


Accordingly

The universe is within the divine.

The universe participates in the divine.The universe may express aspects of the divine.

The universe does not exhaust the divine.

The divine is not reducible to the physical universe.


The universe participates in, expresses, and possesses a genuinely divine character because it exists within the divine, but the universe is not identical with the divine.


Question

Is everything therefore divine?


Response

No.


Everything exists within the divine, but the divine is not identical with every individual thing.


A thing's participation in the divine does not make that thing identical with the totality of the divine.


Question

If the divine can communicate, how can a person distinguish divine communication from their own interpretation?


Response

A person's experience of apparent divine communication and their interpretation of that experience are distinct propositions.


Personal Deism therefore does not treat every claimed revelation as unquestionably divine.


A claim of divine communication remains subject to epistemic evaluation.


The person may reasonably regard an experience as spiritually significant while remaining uncertain about its precise source, meaning, or implications.


A claimed communication that requires cruelty, domination, denial of equal dignity, arbitrary discrimination, or rejection of well-established evidence is presumptively defective within Personal Deism.


The possibility of divine communication therefore does not eliminate the need for reason, discernment, humility, or moral evaluation.


Question
Why does Personal Deism regard the goodness of the divine as necessarily true rather than merely contingently true?

Response
Personal Deism holds that the divine is the ultimate ground of reality and that goodness belongs necessarily to the nature of the ultimate rather than being an external standard imposed upon it. A contingent goodness would mean that the ultimate reality could, in principle, possess a contrary moral nature. Personal Deism rejects this possibility as inconsistent with its conception of the divine as the ultimate ground of objective goodness. This is a metaphysical commitment of the system, not an empirical conclusion, and remains open to philosophical criticism.


Objection

You have simply defined the divine as good.


Response

The divine is not merely defined as good by linguistic stipulation. Personal Deism makes the substantive metaphysical claim that goodness belongs to the nature of the divine.


Objection
Isn't saying that the divine is good merely a definition rather than an argument?


Response
Personal Deism does not claim that the proposition "the divine is good" becomes true merely because the system categorizes the divine using the word "good." Rather, the claim is that the divine is understood as the metaphysical ground of objective goodness. Theoretically, if the divine were capable of being contrary to goodness in its essential nature, then the divine would not be the ultimate ground of the objective goodness affirmed by Personal Deism.

Thus, the necessity is metaphysical rather than merely verbal: the divine could not cease to be good while remaining the kind of ultimate reality that Personal Deism calls the divine. The claim is therefore about the nature of ultimate reality, not about a voluntary command or an arbitrary definition.

This does not constitute an independent empirical proof that the divine exists or that the divine is good. It is a metaphysical claim concerning what the divine would have to be if the divine exists as the ultimate ground of objective goodness.


The claim is not that goodness is good because the divine is called good; rather, the claim is that objective goodness is grounded in the necessary nature of ultimate reality, and Personal Deism identifies that ultimate reality as the divine.


Question

If a non-person sentient creature is already entitled to protection from hunger, thirst, discomfort, pain, disease, restriction of natural capacities, and fear or distress, what morally significant protection does personhood add?


Response

Personhood entails the capacity for moral responsibility, but the extent of an individual's actual responsibilities is proportionate to that individual's capacities and circumstances.
