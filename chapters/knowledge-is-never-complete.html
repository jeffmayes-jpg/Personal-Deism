Knowledge Is Never Complete


Every belief should remain open to refinement in light of better evidence, deeper reasoning, or more profound experience.


No finite body of evidence is necessarily immune to reconsideration. New evidence may strengthen, weaken, qualify, or overturn a previously justified belief.


A belief's being widely accepted does not make it true, and a belief's being unpopular does not make it false.


There is always more to learn, about any subject.


A person may possess justified and well-supported knowledge while remaining capable of error. Certainty is not required for rational belief, and confidence should correspond to the strength of the available justification.


A proposition does not become true because it is believed, accepted by a community, supported by authority, or experienced as true. Beliefs are evaluated according to whether they correspond to reality and whether the believer has adequate justification for holding them.


Our perception and understanding of reality are not identical to reality itself. We experience reality through perception, but perception is not itself reality. Our understanding of what we perceive is a subjective construct and therefore remains subject to error, revision, and refinement. Consequently, confidence in a proposition should be proportionate to the evidence supporting it rather than to the certainty with which it is perceived.


Consensus can be evidence without being proof. Agreement among observers may increase confidence in an interpretation, but agreement alone does not establish that the interpretation corresponds to reality.


A claim itself establishes little. At a minimum, the making of a claim establishes the fact that the claim was made; it does not, by itself, establish the truth of what was claimed.


An accusation does not constitute proof, nor does an assertion constitute evidence of the truth of the proposition asserted. An accusation establishes that an allegation has been made; an assertion establishes that a proposition has been stated. Neither establishes the truth of its content without supporting evidence.


The burden of justification applies to both affirmation and negation. Saying that a proposition is true does not make it true, and saying that it is false does not make it false. Each position must be evaluated according to the evidence supporting it.


Intellectual humility requires recognizing the limits of one's knowledge, distinguishing confidence from certainty, acknowledging relevant evidence against one's position, and remaining willing to revise beliefs when warranted.


Intellectual humility does not require treating all beliefs as equally justified.


Levels of Confidence of Knowledge

Certainty

A proposition whose justification is so overwhelming that rational doubt is no longer warranted.

Very High Confidence

Supported by exceptionally strong and convergent evidence.

High Confidence

Supported by strong evidence and reasoning with no significant defeaters.

Provisional Acceptance

Reasonably supported but subject to substantial uncertainty.

Hypothesis

A plausible explanatory proposal requiring further investigation.

Speculation

A possibility lacking sufficient justification for confident belief.

Unknown

Insufficient evidence to rationally favor a conclusion.


A person should proportion belief to the strength of the available justification.

Conclusive evidence → Certainty, where appropriate
Strong evidence → Very High Confidence
Moderate evidence → Hypothesis or Provisional Acceptance
Weak evidence → Speculation
Insufficient justification → Unknown or suspension of judgment.


A person should actively seek evidence that could disconfirm their beliefs rather than merely seeking evidence that confirms them.


The process by which truth is sought is

Reasoning

Provisional belief

Revision or increased confidence
Continued openness to correction


Intellectual humility means recognizing the limits of one's knowledge without treating knowledge as impossible.


A person should distinguish:

• what they know;

• what they reasonably believe;

• what they infer;

• what they suspect;

• what they hope;

• and what they do not know.


Uncertainty should be acknowledged where it is genuine. Certainty should not be manufactured merely to eliminate discomfort.


Question

Doesn't this make certainty impossible?


Response

It distinguishes between confidence and infallibility. One may hold well-supported beliefs while remaining open to refinement.


Question

What gives us sufficient reason to hold a belief before revision becomes necessary?


Response

Revision may occur as new evidence is presented. Revision becomes necessary when there is evidence with a higher degree of confidence.


Personal Deism therefore rejects the idea that openness to revision requires perpetual neutrality. A person may reasonably reach a conclusion while remaining open to correction.


Truth takes precedence over comfort, loyalty, tradition, authority, and personal preference.


Because perception, interpretation, and consensus can all be mistaken, the pursuit of truth requires

willingness to reconsider even conclusions that appear obvious or widely accepted.
