Greater Capability Creates Greater Responsibility, Not Greater Worth, Authority, Or License.


Greater capability creates greater responsibility, while equal dignity remains universal. This responsibility is always limited by the person's own equal dignity, legitimate rights, reasonable capacity, and legitimate responsibilities to self and others.


A person's reasonable ability to affect the flourishing, freedom, safety, dignity, or well-being of others creates a corresponding responsibility to exercise that ability wisely, justly, and compassionately.


Capability may arise from knowledge, understanding, skill, physical ability, material resources, authority, influence, opportunity, or access to means capable of producing significant effects.


Capability does not determine a person’s worth. Every person possesses equal intrinsic dignity regardless of ability, status, wealth, intelligence, health, or circumstance. Differences in capability therefore create differences in responsibility, not differences in value.


Capability establishes the potential for responsibility; the circumstances, seriousness of the need, foreseeability of consequences, cost of intervention, and legitimate claims of others determine the extent and urgency of that responsibility.


Responsibility is proportionate to capability, understanding, opportunity, and circumstance. A person cannot reasonably be held responsible for accomplishing what they genuinely cannot accomplish, nor for exercising a capability whose existence or relevant circumstances they could not reasonably know.


However, when a person possesses the ability to prevent serious harm, protect another person's legitimate freedom or safety, or substantially contribute to personal flourishing, that capability may create a corresponding moral responsibility to consider and, when reasonably possible, undertake such action.


The greater the foreseeable consequences of exercising or neglecting a capability, the greater the care required in its exercise.


Responsibility is proportionate to relevant capability, not capability in the abstract.


Capability therefore creates responsibility in at least three fundamental ways:

Capacity to Act
The ability to produce an outcome creates responsibility for how that ability is exercised.

Capacity to Prevent Harm
The ability to reasonably prevent serious harm creates a responsibility to consider prevention when doing so does not require an unreasonable sacrifice or violate another person's legitimate rights.

Capacity to Promote Flourishing
The ability to substantially improve the lives, relationships, communities, or natural environment of others creates a moral opportunity and, in circumstances of significant need, may create a moral responsibility to assist.


Capability acquired involuntarily does not eliminate responsibility for its exercise. A capability received through birth, circumstance, inheritance, accident, education, or other means remains a responsibility once the individual possesses sufficient understanding and agency to exercise it.

Capability does not, however, create unlimited obligation. A person may pursue their own legitimate flourishing, establish reasonable boundaries, decline unreasonable demands, and preserve the resources necessary for their own well-being and responsibilities.


Capability creates responsibility, not superiority.


The most capable person is not thereby the most valuable person. The person with the greatest resources does not possess greater dignity than the person with the fewest. Rather, greater capability creates a greater opportunity—and therefore potentially a greater responsibility—to contribute to the flourishing of others.


In Personal Deism, capability is therefore understood not primarily as a privilege of power, but as a corresponding opportunity for service. Greater ability should lead not to greater domination, but to greater stewardship.


A person has a strong duty to prevent grave and foreseeable harm when they can do so effectively, without violating another’s rights, and without imposing a comparable threat to their own basic flourishing or legitimate responsibilities. This duty concerns morally serious circumstances and does not create a general requirement to intervene in every instance of preventable harm.


Principle

Responsibility requires a morally relevant capability.


Not every ability creates a significant moral obligation.


A capability creates significant responsibility when:

1. The person possesses the relevant capability.

2. The person reasonably understands its existence and consequences.

3. The capability is relevant to a legitimate moral interest.

4. The potential benefit or harm is sufficiently significant.

5. The person has a reasonable opportunity to exercise the capability.

6. Exercising the capability would not require an unreasonable sacrifice or violate another person's legitimate rights.


Responsibility therefore does not increase merely because a person possesses more abilities in the abstract.


Responsibility increases according to the morally relevant consequences that the person can reasonably understand and affect.


Capability → relevant opportunity → foreseeable consequence → proportionate responsibility.


Question

What is the difference between responsibility, obligation, and duty?


Response

Personal Deism distinguishes these concepts while recognizing that they may overlap.


Question

Does greater capability make someone morally superior?


Responsibility is the condition of being appropriately accountable for the exercise or consequences of a capability.


An obligation is a morally required action or restraint arising from a legitimate moral reason.

A duty is a sufficiently specific obligation attached to a particular relationship, role, circumstance, or moral claim.


Capability creates the potential for responsibility.


Circumstance determines whether that responsibility becomes an obligation.


The seriousness of the moral interest, the person's knowledge, opportunity, capability, foreseeable consequences, and legitimate claims of others determine the strength of the obligation.


Therefore:

Capability → responsibility

Relevant circumstance → obligation

Specific role or relationship → potentially specific duty

Greater capability does not automatically create an unlimited duty to serve everyone.

Responsibility remains proportionate to capability, knowledge, opportunity, circumstance, and the legitimate claims of all affected persons.


Question

Why does intrinsic value generate a moral obligation?


Response

Intrinsic value establishes that the thing possessing that value is morally considerable for its own sake.

If a person possesses intrinsic value, then that person's interests, freedom, flourishing, and continued existence cannot be treated as morally irrelevant merely because another person prefers otherwise.


A moral obligation arises when an agent has a sufficient reason to act or refrain from acting in light of the legitimate moral claims of another.


Intrinsic dignity therefore does not mean that every interest of every person must always prevail. It means that no person's dignity may be treated as morally irrelevant.


Justice requires that the legitimate claims of persons be considered according to consistent principles rather than according to preference, power, status, wealth, or social usefulness.


Intrinsic dignity establishes the moral standing of the person.

Rights establish certain protected claims of that person.

Justice governs conflicts between legitimate claims.

Responsibility concerns how one's capabilities should be exercised toward those claims.


Therefore:

Intrinsic dignity → moral standing → legitimate moral claims → obligations of appropriate respect and protection.


Question

Does greater capability make someone morally superior?


Response

No. Capability and dignity are fundamentally different. A person with extraordinary abilities and a person with very limited abilities possess equal intrinsic dignity. The difference lies in what each person is capable of doing, and therefore potentially in what each person is responsible for doing.


Question

Does greater capability mean that the most capable people must do everything for everyone else?


Response

No. Capability creates responsibility, not unlimited obligation. Responsibility must remain proportionate to the capability possessed, the circumstances involved, the seriousness of the need, the cost of intervention, and the legitimate rights and responsibilities of others.


Question

What if a person has the ability to help but chooses not to?


Response

The moral significance depends upon the circumstances. A minor opportunity to help does not necessarily create a significant obligation. However, when a person possesses a substantial ability to prevent serious harm or preserve another person's fundamental freedom or dignity at reasonable cost, deliberately refusing to act may constitute a moral failure.


Question

Does this mean wealthy people have greater intrinsic worth?


Response

No. Intrinsic worth and responsibility are separate concepts. Every person possesses equal dignity. Greater resources merely provide greater capability and therefore potentially greater responsibility.


Question

Does capability create a legal duty?


Response

Not necessarily.


Personal Deism distinguishes moral obligation from legal obligation. Capability may create a moral responsibility without creating a legitimate basis for government coercion. Government may compel conduct only where the requirements of justice, protection of rights, or legitimate public safety justify such intervention.


Question

What if helping another person requires violating someone else's freedom?


Response

Capability does not override fundamental rights. Responsibility must be exercised consistently with justice. One person's capability to benefit another does not automatically authorize coercion, deprivation, or violation of a third person's legitimate freedom.


Question

What if a person does not know that they possess the capability to help?


Response

Responsibility is related not only to capability but also to understanding. A person cannot reasonably be held responsible for failing to exercise a capability whose existence or relevant circumstances they could not reasonably have known. As knowledge increases, responsibility may increase accordingly.


Question

Does a person have a responsibility to develop greater capabilities?


Response

Potentially, but not without limit. When a person can reasonably acquire knowledge, skill, or other capability that would substantially improve their ability to protect, provide, nurture, cultivate, or otherwise promote flourishing, acquiring that capability may itself become a moral responsibility. However, such responsibility remains proportional to the person's circumstances and legitimate obligations to themselves and others.


Question

Does greater power always mean greater responsibility?


Response

Greater power generally increases the potential consequences of one's actions and therefore increases the need for responsible exercise. However, responsibility depends upon more than power alone. Knowledge, intention, opportunity, foreseeable consequences, circumstances, and legitimate rights must also be considered.


Question

What is the relationship between capability and responsibility?


Response

Capability establishes the possibility of action; understanding establishes awareness of its significance; freedom establishes meaningful choice; and responsibility concerns the appropriate exercise of that capability.


Question

Is refusing to help morally equivalent to causing harm?


Response

No.


Personal Deism distinguishes between:

1. Causing harm,

2. Failing to prevent harm,

3. Failing to provide assistance,

4. Respecting a person's decision not to accept assistance.


These circumstances may carry different moral responsibilities.


A person who directly causes serious harm generally bears a stronger responsibility than a person who merely fails to prevent harm.

However, a failure to act may become morally serious when the person possesses a substantial capability to prevent grave and foreseeable harm, understands the circumstances, has a reasonable opportunity to intervene, and can do so without violating another person's legitimate rights or imposing an unreasonable sacrifice.


The moral significance of inaction therefore depends upon capability, knowledge, opportunity, foreseeable consequences, circumstance, and the legitimate claims of all persons affected.


Question

What is the difference between a right and a morally significant interest?


Response

A morally significant interest is something whose protection or fulfillment has moral importance.


A right is a morally protected claim that places corresponding limits upon the conduct of others or upon legitimate authority.


Not every interest necessarily constitutes an identical right.


Personal Deism therefore distinguishes:

Moral interest → something worthy of consideration.

Right → a protected moral claim imposing corresponding duties or restrictions.

Legal right → a right recognized and enforceable within the legitimate authority of law.


A creature may possess morally significant interests without possessing the same rights as a person.


Equal dignity establishes equal basic rights among persons, but equal dignity does not require identical rights in every circumstance.
