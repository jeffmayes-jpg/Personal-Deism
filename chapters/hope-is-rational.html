Hope Is Rational


Hope need not be directed toward oneself. Personal hope and hope for others are distinct. Even when a person has no personal hope, hope for the well-being, flourishing, or moral progress of others can remain rational and meaningful.

Hope is rational when it is grounded in the genuine possibility of improvement and informed by evidence concerning the means by which improvement may occur.


Hope does not require certainty that a desired outcome will occur.


Hope may remain rational in circumstances where success is uncertain if there are sufficient reasons to believe that constructive action can improve the situation.


Personal Deism therefore distinguishes:

Hope — rational orientation toward a possible good.

Optimism — expectation that a favorable outcome is likely.

Certainty — justified confidence that an outcome will occur.

Hope does not require optimism or certainty.

Hope becomes irrational when it deliberately ignores strong evidence that the hoped-for outcome is impossible or when it substitutes desire for evidence.


The absence of personal hope does not necessarily imply the absence of hope itself. One's capacity to hope can be directed outward.


Question

Why is hope rational?


Response

Human beings possess demonstrated capacities for learning, cooperation, correction, creativity, and moral improvement.


These capacities provide grounds for believing that improvement is possible even when it is not guaranteed.


Hope therefore concerns possibility and purposeful action rather than certainty about the future.


Objection

Human history is mostly violence and suffering.


Response

History also demonstrates moral, scientific, and humanitarian progress. Hope is grounded in humanity's demonstrated capacity to improve through education and cooperation.
