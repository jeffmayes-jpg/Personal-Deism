Not every moral violation is a legal violation


Moral obligations concern how a person ought to live. Legal obligations concern conduct that may legitimately be compelled by public authority. A failure to cultivate virtue is not, by itself, grounds for coercive governmental intervention unless the conduct violates the rights or legitimate freedoms of another person or creates a sufficiently serious threat requiring protective action.


Principle

Moral Obligation Does Not Automatically Establish Legal Authority


A moral obligation and a legal obligation are distinct.


A person may have a genuine moral responsibility to act without the government possessing legitimate authority to compel that action.


Government coercion requires an additional justification.


That justification must arise from the protection of legitimate rights, prevention of sufficiently serious harm, or another legitimate public interest consistent with equal dignity and fundamental rights.

Therefore:

Moral obligation → establishes what a person ought to do.

Legal obligation → establishes what legitimate authority may require under law.

Moral failure does not automatically constitute a legal violation.


The purpose of law is not to make every person virtuous. Its legitimate purpose is to protect persons, rights, freedom, public safety, and the conditions necessary for people to coexist and flourish.


Question

Can something be morally wrong without being legally prohibited?


Response

Yes.


Personal Deism explicitly recognizes that morality is broader than law.


A person may have a moral obligation to be compassionate, generous, courageous, merciful, patient, or helpful without government possessing legitimate authority to compel the corresponding conduct.


Law should protect the conditions within which virtue can develop rather than attempt to manufacture virtue through unlimited coercion.
