Moral Growth Is the Objective Purpose of Personal Existence


Moral growth is the progressive realization of one's moral potential. Progress does not necessarily mean linear. “Progressive realization” means development toward realization. Moral potential is the capacity of a person to develop in understanding, character, judgment, and expression of the good.


Moral growth is not merely something persons happen to value; it is what existence is objectively for. Moral growth is the progressive realization of the human capacity to participate in and express the good. Therefore, moral growth is the objective purpose of personal existence.


The objective purpose of personal existence is not merely pleasure or survival, but the continual development of character, understanding, and virtue. Virtues include: Wisdom, Honesty, Courage, Compassion, Justice (acting fairly and respecting the rights of others), Temperance, Humility, Responsibility, Perseverance and Mercy.


Basic flourishing provides the conditions in which moral development can occur.

Personal flourishing develops one's capabilities.

Relational flourishing provides relationships through which virtue can be practiced.

Moral flourishing is the development of character and virtue.

Transcendent flourishing is participation in or orientation toward the divine.


Moral growth is the primary objective teleological purpose of personal existence, but it does not exhaust the goods that constitute human flourishing. Other forms of flourishing are intrinsically valuable while also providing conditions, relationships, experiences, and opportunities through which moral growth may occur.


Moral development is not measured solely by outward conformity to rules. It includes the development of motives, judgment, character, understanding, and the ability to recognize and respond appropriately to the legitimate claims of others.


Justice governs rights; compassion governs their humane application.


Goodness is not a standard external to the divine, nor is goodness good merely because the divine declares it so. Rather, the divine is good (foundational commitment), and moral goodness is an expression of that divine aspect.


Question

Does Personal Deism claim that moral growth is the purpose of all existence, including non-personal existence?


Response

Personal Deism distinguishes the purpose of personal existence from the purpose of existence as a whole.


The philosophy's strongest teleological claim concerns personal existence.


Persons possess the distinctive capacities for moral understanding, intentional choice, relationship, reflection, and development of character. These capacities are understood as ordered toward the good.

Therefore, moral growth constitutes the objective purpose of personal existence.

The broader claim that all existence possesses a single purpose requires a more comprehensive metaphysical argument and is not established merely by observing that persons possess moral capacities.


Accordingly, Personal Deism holds most directly:

Moral growth is the objective purpose of personal existence.


The broader purpose of personal existence is understood within the divine teleological structure of reality but is not reduced to the moral development of persons alone.


This distinction prevents the philosophy from attributing personal moral development as the literal purpose of every non-personal feature of reality.


Question

Why does moral growth constitute the objective purpose of personal existence rather than merely a valuable activity?


Response

Personal Deism distinguishes between something being valuable and something constituting an objective purpose.


A capacity may be possessed without being fully realized. The existence of a capacity therefore does not, by itself, establish its moral purpose.


The philosophy instead holds that objective purpose arises from the proper relation between a being's nature, its capacities, and the good toward which those capacities are ordered.


Persons possess distinctive capacities for understanding, choosing, relating, reflecting upon goodness, and intentionally expressing moral character.


If those capacities are objectively ordered toward goodness, then their proper development constitutes a genuine moral end rather than merely a subjective preference.


Therefore:

Persons possess capacities for moral understanding and expression.Those capacities are ordered toward objective goodness. Their proper realization constitutes a form of flourishing. The progressive realization of those capacities is moral growth. Moral growth therefore constitutes an objective purpose of personal existence.


This conclusion depends upon the teleological premise that capacities have objectively proper ends. If the metaphysical premises are true, moral growth follows as the objective purpose of personal existence.


The philosophy therefore does not claim to have demonstrated teleology independently of its metaphysical framework.


Question
Why does Personal Deism accept objective teleological ordering?

Response
Personal Deism accepts objective teleological ordering as a foundational metaphysical commitment: beings possess natures that are properly ordered toward the realization of goods constitutive of those natures. In persons, this includes the capacity to understand, choose, develop, and express moral goodness.

This commitment is not presented as something demonstrated independently by empirical science. Rather, it is a metaphysical interpretation of the relationship among nature, objective goodness, and purpose. If objective goodness is real and persons possess capacities that are properly directed toward its recognition and realization, then moral development is not merely a subjective preference or an accidental consequence of existence. It constitutes an objective end toward which personal nature is properly ordered.

Personal Deism therefore regards teleology as providing the metaphysical grounding for its claim that moral growth is the objective purpose of personal existence. The conclusion is conditional upon the foundational commitments concerning objective goodness, personal nature, and teleological ordering. Personal Deism does not claim that the existence of teleology can be established independently of those commitments.


Question

What kind of participation does Personal Deism mean, and why does that kind of participation necessarily communicate the particular divine attribute of goodness to persons?


Response

Participation is ontological and involves participation in divine goodness. Personal Deism understands persons as possessing a genuine ontological relationship to the divine that is constitutive of personhood rather than merely incidental. Because the divine is understood as the ultimate ground of objective goodness, participation in the divine is consequently participation in the good that constitutes the divine nature. The person therefore does not merely stand in an external relationship to goodness; participation in the divine establishes an intrinsic relation to objective goodness.

This does not mean that persons are identical with the divine, that persons are portions of the divine, or that every action performed by a person is good. It means that the person's existence and intrinsic moral nature are grounded in a genuine relation to the divine and, therefore, to the goodness that belongs necessarily to the divine nature.

Personal Deism does not claim to prove ontological participation independently of its foundational metaphysical commitments. The claim is instead an internal metaphysical consequence of the relationship among the divine, divine goodness, and personhood.


Objection

Happiness is existence's purpose.


Response

Pleasure and happiness are valuable, but they are viewed as consequences of virtuous living rather than existence's ultimate purpose.


Question

Why these particular virtues?


Response

Each virtue contributes to a person’s flourishing and reflects qualities associated with the divine. Together they provide a balanced model of character.


Question

Why should possessing moral potential mean that realizing it is the objective purpose of personal existence?


Response

A thing's objective purpose is determined by the good toward which its nature is properly ordered. Persons possess the distinctive capacity to understand, choose, develop, and express moral goodness. Therefore the realization and cultivation of that capacity constitutes the objective moral end of personal existence.


Persons participate in the divine → the divine is good → persons possess a capacity to participate in and express that goodness → realization of that capacity is therefore ordered toward the good. This remains a metaphysical premise, rather than something that can be established independently of the system's foundational commitments.


Question

If moral growth is the objective purpose of personal existence, are all other activities subordinate to moral improvement?


Response

No.


Moral growth is a central and irreducible dimension of flourishing, but flourishing is not reducible to constant moral effort.


Rest, friendship, play, contemplation, creativity, enjoyment, learning, recovery, and ordinary experience may themselves constitute genuine forms of flourishing.

An activity does not need to appear directly productive in order to possess moral or personal value.


Moral growth concerns the development of character, understanding, judgment, and virtue. Those qualities can be developed through both deliberate effort and the ordinary experiences through which a person exists.


Moral growth therefore gives existence an objective direction without reducing existence to continuous self-improvement.


Question

Why accept the metaphysical premise that a capacity has an objectively proper end?


Response

Personal Deism adopts as a teleological premise that beings and their capacities have objectively proper ends, and that the proper realization of a capacity is relevant to understanding its flourishing.


This is a metaphysical premise, not a conclusion derived from the bare existence of a capacity.


A teleological premise does not claim that every capacity ought to be exercised maximally or continuously. It claims that capacities have objective ends when those capacities are constitutively related to genuine goods within the nature of the being possessing them.


Question

Does this mean a person is morally deficient whenever they are not actively improving?"


Response

Growth can include: learning, reflection, practicing virtue, correcting mistakes, developing judgment, repairing relationships, resisting temptation, becoming more capable of helping others, understanding one's own limitations, or other areas. Sometimes rest, recovery, contemplation, play, friendship, and ordinary enjoyment can support moral growth without being directly "productive”.


Objection

You've established that moral growth is valuable. You haven't established that it is the objective purpose of personal existence.


Response

Persons participate in objective goodness; their distinctive capacity is to understand and intentionally express that goodness; therefore the realization of that capacity constitutes the objective moral purpose of personal existence.


Question

Why does possessing a capacity establish that its realization is the objective purpose of the being?


Response

The claim that moral potential has an objective end is a metaphysical premise of Personal Deism: beings are properly ordered toward the realization of the goods constitutive of their nature. Human beings possess distinctive capacities for understanding, choosing, developing, and intentionally expressing moral goodness. Because those capacities are ordered toward objective goodness, their proper realization constitutes an objective end of human life. Moral growth is therefore not merely something humans happen to value; it is the progressive realization of a capacity whose proper end is objective goodness.


The relevant claim is therefore not that every capacity automatically establishes an objective purpose. Rather, Personal Deism holds that a capacity has an objective purpose when that capacity is constitutively ordered toward a genuine good within the nature of the being possessing it. Personal moral capacities are understood in this way because they involve the ability to recognize, choose, develop, and express objective goodness.


The teleological conclusion therefore depends upon two metaphysical premises: that objective goodness is real and that human moral capacities are genuinely ordered toward that goodness. Given those premises, the realization of personal moral potential constitutes the objective moral end of personal existence.


The necessity of divine goodness follows from Personal Deism's conception of the divine as the ultimate ground of objective goodness rather than as a being that merely happens to possess goodness. If the divine could be fundamentally evil, goodness would not be grounded in the divine and the claim that moral reality is an expression of the divine would fail. Personal Deism therefore treats necessary divine goodness as a foundational metaphysical commitment rather than as a conclusion derived from contingent observations.


This does not mean that every event occurring within the universe is good, nor that every action attributed to the divine is thereby justified. Human interpretations of divine action remain fallible and subject to rational and ethical examination.


This conclusion is not presented as an empirical deduction from the mere existence of a capacity. It follows from Personal Deism's metaphysical account of human nature, objective goodness, and teleological order.


Question

Why does a capacity's proper realization necessarily constitute its objective purpose?


Response

It is a teleological premise, not something that follows from the mere existence of a capacity.


Question
Can it be demonstrated that moral growth is the objective purpose of personal existence?


Response
Yes, conditionally within the metaphysical framework of Personal Deism.


The demonstration begins with the foundational premises that the divine exists, that the divine is good, that persons participate in the divine, and that this participation is constitutive of personhood.


If the divine is intrinsically good, and persons participate in that goodness, then the realization and expression of goodness are objectively relevant to the nature of persons.


Persons possess capacities through which they can understand, choose, develop, and express values. These capacities make possible the intentional development of character and conduct toward greater conformity with the good.


Moral growth is therefore not merely a socially preferred behavior, a subjective preference, or an accidental consequence of human development. It is the progressive realization of a person's capacity to understand and participate in the good.


The reasoning is:

The divine is good.
Persons participate in the divine.
Persons therefore participate in the good.
Persons possess capacities through which participation in the good can be expressed and

developed.
The development of those capacities toward the good constitutes moral growth.
Therefore, moral growth is the objective fulfillment of personal nature.
Therefore, moral growth is the objective purpose of personal existence.


This conclusion does not mean that every person is morally mature, that every action is morally good, or that every person consciously recognizes this purpose. An objective purpose does not cease to exist because a person fails to understand, pursue, or fulfill it.


Nor does the conclusion mean that every person must develop in exactly the same way. Because persons possess different capabilities, circumstances, experiences, and legitimate values, moral growth may be expressed differently by different persons.


Moral growth therefore concerns direction rather than uniformity: becoming more capable of understanding, choosing, and expressing the good appropriate to one's circumstances.


The conclusion is consequently a metaphysical and teleological claim rather than an empirical claim. Its validity depends upon the foundational premises of Personal Deism, particularly the existence and goodness of the divine, the participation of persons in the divine, and the objective reality of goodness.


Question
Why does participation in the divine imply a purpose of moral growth rather than merely the possession of intrinsic value?


Response
Intrinsic value establishes that persons are worthy of moral consideration. Participation in the divine establishes a deeper relationship to the good.


If persons merely possessed value, it would follow that they should be respected. But if persons also participate in an intrinsically good reality, then their capacities for understanding, choosing, and expressing the good have an objective direction.


Moral growth is the process by which a person increasingly realizes that direction.


Thus:

Intrinsic value → establishes moral worth.
Participation in the divine → establishes relationship to the good.
Personal capacities → make participation in the good expressible.
Development of those capacities toward the good → constitutes moral growth.


Therefore, moral growth follows not merely from the fact that persons have value, but from what persons are understood to be in relation to the good.


Question
Does this prove that moral growth is the purpose of personal existence to someone who rejects the metaphysical premises of Personal Deism?


Response
No. A philosophical demonstration cannot establish its conclusion independently of premises that its argument requires.


A person who rejects the existence of the divine, the objective reality of goodness, or the participation of persons in the divine will not be compelled by this argument alone to accept its conclusion.


The claim is therefore demonstrated conditionally within the metaphysical framework of Personal Deism:


If the foundational metaphysical premises are true, then moral growth follows as the objective purpose of personal existence.


The philosophical task is consequently divided into two questions:

  1. Whether the foundational metaphysical premises are justified.

  2. Whether moral growth follows logically from those premises.

Personal Deism holds that the answer to both questions is affirmative.


Question:
If moral growth is the objective purpose of personal existence, how should Personal Deism evaluate a person who achieves exceptional moral growth but has little opportunity or ability to exercise that growth through action, compared with someone who performs many apparently virtuous actions but undergoes little genuine moral development?

Response

The performance of virtuous acts is not the goal in and of itself, and does not guarantee Moral Growth, especially if they are performed specifically for that purpose. Indeed, are they even virtuous then?


Question

If moral growth is not measured primarily by virtuous acts, what constitutes sufficient evidence that genuine moral growth has occurred?

Response

Genuine Moral Growth is demonstrated through such things as changes in character, understanding, intentions, judgment, relationships, and orientation toward the good.
